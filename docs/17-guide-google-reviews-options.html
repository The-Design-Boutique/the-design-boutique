Pulling Google Reviews onto the Website

Options, costs and what we would need from you  |  The Design Boutique  |  July 27, 2026

The testimonials on /more-testimonials are currently hardcoded: the twelve reviews are typed into the page, and the 5.0 rating badge is a static widget. If a client leaves a new Google review, nothing on the site changes until someone edits it by hand.

This document sets out the three realistic ways to make that page pull from Google automatically, what each costs, and what we would need from you to build it. There is a clear recommendation at the end.

Short answer on cost: nothing needs to be bought

Both options we would actually recommend are free. Google's Business Profile API costs nothing to use, and the fallback option is free at this site's traffic level.

The only option on this page that costs money is the one currently in use. The TrustIndex widget on the site today is on a free tier that withholds two features this project actually needs, so keeping it properly would mean paying $65 to $349 a year. Moving to Google directly removes that cost rather than adding one.

The one rule that decides this

Google's Maps Platform terms prohibit storing review text. Section 3.2.3(a) explicitly bars anyone from "copy and save business names, addresses, or user reviews", and the only caching Google permits for the Places API is latitude and longitude, for 30 days. Review text has no such allowance.

That single rule rules out the obvious approach of syncing reviews into the CMS on a schedule, unless we use the Business Profile API, which has its own separate and more generous 30 day allowance. This is why the recommendation below is not the cheapest or the fastest option.

Option 1: Google Business Profile API RECOMMENDED

This is Google's official API for a business to manage its own profile. Because you own the profile, Google grants far more access than the public Places API does.

What it gives us

  • Every review, not just five. Paginated 50 at a time, with the total count and average rating.
  • Sorting by rating or by most recent.
  • The ability to reply to reviews from the site's admin, if you ever want that. Replies post back to Google.
  • Permission to cache for up to 30 days, which means the page can be fast and does not have to call Google on every visit.

What it costs

Nothing. Google states plainly that the Business Profile API is available at no charge.

There is no per-request fee, no monthly minimum, and no credit card required for the API itself.

What we would need from you

  1. Confirmation the Google Business Profile is verified and has been active for at least 60 days. Google requires this before it will even consider an access request. Almost certainly already true, but it has to be confirmed.
  2. Manager access to the profile for a Google account we nominate. Google explicitly recommends that agencies and partners be added as managers and never as owners, so you keep full ownership and can revoke our access at any time. Adding a manager takes about a minute in the Business Profile settings.
  3. Someone with owner or manager status to submit the access request form. Google requires the submitting email address to already be on the profile. We can prepare the entire submission for you, but the send has to come from an account on the profile.
  4. A decision on the Google Cloud project. Either we create one under our account and hand it over at the end of the engagement, or we use one under yours. Either works; yours is tidier long term.

You do not need to buy anything for this option.

This is not needed for the site to go live

Nothing here blocks anything. The website launches perfectly well without it, and this can be started at any point, before launch or a year afterwards, without anything having to be rebuilt. If you decide you want it, the only reason to begin sooner rather than later is the approval wait described below, which is out of everybody's hands.

How to request access, step by step

Four steps. Two of them are a few minutes of clicking, one is a form, and the last is waiting. Everything except the form can be done by us if you would rather; the form itself has to be sent by somebody who is already an owner or manager on the Business Profile, because Google checks that the sending address is on the profile.

1Check the profile qualifies

Google will only consider a request if the Business Profile is verified, active, and has been for at least 60 days, and the business has a website listed on it. All of that is almost certainly already true; it just has to be confirmed before anything is submitted.

2Create a Google Cloud project and note its number

https://console.cloud.google.com

Sign in, click Create project, give it a name such as “TDB Website Reviews”, and confirm. Nothing is charged; a project on its own costs nothing.

Then open the project’s Dashboard and find the Project number on the Project info card. It is a long string of digits. Copy it, because the form asks for it and the request cannot be submitted without it.

3Submit the access request

https://support.google.com/business/contact/api_default

Choose Application for Basic API Access and fill in the form. It asks for the project number from the previous step, the business details, and the website address.

Send it from an email address that is already an owner or manager on the Business Profile. This is the one part that cannot be delegated: Google matches the sending address against the profile and rejects anything else. We can prepare every answer for you in advance; the send has to come from you.

4Wait, and check the quota

Google reviews each request by hand and replies by email. They do not publish a guaranteed turnaround, and in practice it is commonly a couple of weeks. Nothing can speed it up, and chasing it does not help.

You can check without waiting for the email. In the Cloud console, look at the quota for the Business Profile API: 0 requests per minute means it has not been approved yet, and 300 means it has.

What happens after approval

The building is the small part. Once access is granted, connecting it to the website and showing the reviews is a short piece of work. The wait above is the long pole by a wide margin, which is the only argument for starting it early rather than leaving it until you want it.

Option 2: Places UI Kit FALLBACK

Google provides a drop-in web component that renders reviews for any business. No permission and no relationship with the business is required, just an API key.

Reviews returnedFive, maximum. Google picks which five by its own relevance ranking, and they can change between page loads. There is no way to sort, filter, choose, or page through to the rest.
CostBilled at $1.00 per 1,000 requests, with the first 10,000 per month free. For a site at this traffic level that is realistically free.
Needed from youNothing. No account access, no approval.
Design controlLimited. It is Google's component. We can style it through the properties Google exposes, but we cannot restructure it, and Google's terms forbid removing or obscuring their attribution and links.
Worth knowing

There is a second, older way to do this: calling the Places API directly and building the markup ourselves. We do not recommend it. It is capped at the same five reviews, costs $25 per 1,000 requests with only 1,000 free per month, and because review text cannot legally be stored, every single page view would trigger a paid API call. The UI Kit does the same job for a twenty fifth of the price.

Two hidden costs in staying on the free TrustIndex tier
  • No review schema markup. Schema is what lets Google show star ratings in search results. It is withheld from the free tier. Given that a large part of this project is SEO, that is a meaningful thing to be giving up for $65 a year.
  • No lazy loading. The widget script loads immediately and blocks rendering, which works against the Core Web Vitals work in this project. Also withheld from the free tier.

In other words, if the decision is to stay with TrustIndex, the paid tier is effectively required rather than optional. At $65 per year that is a small number, but it should be a deliberate choice rather than a surprise.

Rules we have to follow either way

If Google reviews are displayed on the site, Google's terms impose requirements. These are obligations, not suggestions, and they apply to every option above.

  • Credit the reviewer. Their name, photo and profile link where there is room. At minimum, their photo.
  • Link each review to Google. Visitors must be able to open the original review on Google Maps.
  • Say how the reviews are ordered or filtered. This one is easy to overlook. If the page shows a selection rather than everything, the page has to say so.
  • Show the Google Maps logo when reviews are displayed without a map.
  • Do not edit review text. Reviews go up verbatim, not trimmed or reworded.
  • Reference Google in the site's terms and privacy policy. A small addition we would handle.
One thing to be aware of before deciding

Today the page shows twelve chosen reviews. Pulling from Google means the page reflects whatever is on the profile, including any future negative review. Filtering to show only positive ones is possible, but Google requires that any filtering be disclosed on the page. This is worth a conscious decision rather than a discovery later.

Recommendation

Go with the Business Profile API, and start the access request early

It is the only option that shows every review rather than five, the only one that lets the reviews be styled to match the rest of the site, and it is free. The 30 day caching allowance also means it will not slow the page down.

The only real cost is the wait for Google's approval. Because that wait is the long pole and cannot be shortened, the sensible move is to file the request now and keep the current hardcoded reviews in place until access comes through. Nothing on the site has to change while we wait, and if approval is refused or takes too long, the Places UI Kit is a working fallback that needs nothing from you.
