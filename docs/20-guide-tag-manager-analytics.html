Tag Manager & Analytics

What they are and how they fit together  |  The Design Boutique  |  July 2026

Two Google products with confusingly similar names, doing completely different jobs. Almost all the confusion around them comes from assuming they are the same thing, so it is worth two minutes to get the difference straight.

Google Analytics The report. It counts visitors, records what they looked at, and shows it back to you. This is where you go to find out what happened.
Google Tag Manager The switchboard. It does not count anything. It is a box on the website that other tools get plugged into, so that adding or changing one never needs a developer.
An analogy that holds up

Tag Manager is a power strip. Analytics is one of the things plugged into it. The power strip does not do anything on its own; it just means you can plug something else in later without rewiring the house. If you ever add a Facebook pixel, a LinkedIn tag or a chat widget, they go into the same strip.

Why bother with the switchboard at all?

Because otherwise every small measurement change is a development job. Without Tag Manager, adding a tracking code means editing the website, testing it and deploying it. With it, that becomes a change inside Google, and the website is untouched.

It is set up once. After that it should mostly be left alone.

The one thing to be careful about

Everything plugged into Tag Manager runs on your website, in your visitors’ browsers. Each one makes the site a little slower.

This matters here more than it might elsewhere, because a lot of work went into making this site fast: it went from 57 to 91 out of 100 on a phone. A container stuffed with a dozen marketing tags is the single most common way a fast site quietly becomes a slow one. The rule of thumb is that anything added should have somebody who can say what it is for.

What is set up right now

Tag Manager containerCreated and connected to the site. It loads after the page has appeared, so it does not delay anything a visitor is waiting for.
Google AnalyticsPlugged into it, recording visits on every page.
Form submissionsThe site tells Analytics whenever somebody successfully sends a form, and which form it was.
Phone tapsThe site tells Analytics whenever somebody taps a phone number, and where on the page they tapped it.
Why the form tracking had to be built rather than switched on

Tag Manager can usually detect a form being sent all by itself. It does that by noticing the page reloading, which is what an old-fashioned form does.

The forms on this site do not reload the page, which is why they feel instant. So the usual detection would have found nothing, while appearing to be set up correctly, and you would have had a report showing zero enquiries forever. Instead the site announces each submission directly, at the moment the server accepts it. As a side benefit, that means a submission that failed validation is never counted as an enquiry.

The one thing still missing

Analytics knows those events are happening. It does not yet know that they matter.

In Analytics, an action you care about is called a key event, and it has to be ticked as such by hand. Until that is done, a form submission is recorded the same way as somebody scrolling: as a thing that happened, not as a result.

It is deliberately left undone, because the answer depends on which actions the business counts as a win, and that is the subject of the separate Measuring What Matters questions. Ticking the wrong things is worse than ticking nothing: once a number is being reported as “conversions” and it includes newsletter signups, nobody trusts it again.

Where to look at the numbers

There is an Analytics tab in the editor, in the top row beside Site Speed. It shows the numbers most worth looking at regularly, on one screen, with each one compared against the same stretch of time immediately before it.

  • How many people came, and whether that is up or down
  • Whether they stayed
  • Where they came from: Google, direct, social, a link elsewhere
  • Which pages they read
  • What they did, once actions are marked as mattering

It is deliberately not a rebuild of Analytics. For anything beyond those questions there is a link through to Google Analytics itself, where every report lives.

Why a tab in the editor rather than only a dashboard

Because the moment you want to know how a page is doing is usually the moment you are editing it. Every page also has its own Search tab showing how that specific page performs in Google. A separate dashboard tends to get looked at when somebody remembers to; a tab next to the content gets looked at while the decision is being made.

Questions you might have

Do I need to log in to Tag Manager?

Almost certainly never. It is infrastructure. If something needs adding to it, ask rather than experiment: a wrong change there affects every page at once.

Is this collecting personal information?

Analytics records behaviour rather than identity: pages, sources, devices, roughly where in the world somebody is. It does not know names, and it is not told what anybody typed into a form. When a form is submitted, the site reports only that a submission happened and which form it was.

Why does today never show any figures?

Analytics finishes counting a day a few hours after it ends, so the most recent complete day is yesterday. A missing today is normal and not a fault.

Can I add a Facebook or LinkedIn pixel later?

Yes, and that is exactly what the container is for. Ask before adding one: it is a two-minute job to do properly and a very easy one to do in a way that slows the site down or double-counts everything.

The container loads nothing at all until the new site goes live, so none of the figures in the editor include anybody testing the preview. Correct as of July 2026.
