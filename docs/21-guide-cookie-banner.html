The Cookie Banner

What it does, and the one setting worth knowing  |  The Design Boutique  |  July 2026

Your website now asks every visitor what tracking they are willing to accept, and holds everything non-essential until they answer. This is the same system running on the WordPress sites, rebuilt for the new site.

Most of it needs nothing from you. This guide explains what visitors see, what happens underneath, and the handful of things that are genuinely your decision.

The part that makes this different

Most cookie banners are a formality. They appear, the visitor clicks something, and the tracking carries on either way, because the banner only ever asked the tracking tools to behave. If a tool ignores the request, or was added to the site by a route the banner does not know about, nothing stops it.

This one is built the other way round. It does not ask. It sits in front of the website's connection to the outside world, and when a tracking tool tries to send information anywhere, it refuses to carry the message. The tool is not asked to stand down, it is prevented.

Why that matters to you

If someone adds a Facebook pixel through Tag Manager next year and forgets to tell anyone, it is blocked by default, for everyone, until a visitor agrees to advertising cookies. You do not have to know it was added for it to be handled correctly.

What a visitor sees

On their first visit, a panel appears at the bottom of the screen with three choices.

Accept all Everything is allowed. This is one click, in the most prominent button, because a consent banner that makes agreeing harder than refusing is itself a problem.
Reject all Nothing non-essential runs. Equally prominent, and equally one click, which is a legal requirement in several states.
Manage preferences Opens the list of categories below, so they can allow some and refuse others.

Their answer is remembered for six months. After that, or if you change the policy version (see below), they are asked again. There is a permanent Your Privacy Choices link in the footer, and a small shield button in the bottom corner of every page, so they can change their mind at any time. Both are required: California expects the opt-out to stay findable rather than disappearing along with the banner.

The four categories

Strictly necessary things, such as security and making the contact form work, are always on and are not offered as a choice. They involve no tracking and share nothing with anybody. Everything else is optional, and starts switched off.

Analytics Counting visits and which pages get read. This is Google Analytics. Refusing it means that visitor is not counted in your reports.
Advertising Advert measurement and audience building, such as Google Ads or a Meta pixel. This is the category that shares information with other companies, which is why it gets the most legal attention.
Chat and call tracking Helpful extras rather than tracking for its own sake: a live chat window, or a phone number that records which page the call came from.
Session recording Tools that record what a visitor did on the page, including where they moved and what they typed, and play it back later. Nothing on your site uses this today. The category is listed anyway, so that if one is ever added it is blocked from the start rather than quietly allowed.

The browser signal

Some browsers now send a "do not sell or share my information" signal on the visitor's behalf, before they have clicked anything. California, Colorado and Connecticut all require that signal to be obeyed, so the site obeys it: advertising stays off for those visitors, and stays off even if they press Accept all, because a browser-level refusal is treated as the more deliberate instruction.

They are still shown the banner, and can still switch on the chat widget if they want it. What they cannot accidentally do is undo an opt-out their browser made for them.

Forms

One more rule, which is separate from cookies but part of the same body of law. Any tick box on a form that signs someone up for marketing, texts, or a newsletter must not arrive already ticked. Agreement has to be something the visitor actively does.

The site now enforces this automatically. If a marketing box is ever set up pre-ticked, it is unticked before the visitor sees it. Boxes for terms and conditions or the privacy policy are deliberately left alone, since unticking those would be its own problem.

What you actually control

Where: Sanity, then Site Settings, then the Cookies & privacy tab.

Most of these you will set once and never touch again.

Policy version This is the one worth understanding. Every visitor's answer is filed against the number in this box at the time they gave it. Raise it by one and everybody is asked again, from scratch. That is what you do after changing what the site collects, or adding a new tool that gathers something new. Nothing else re-asks people, so if you make a meaningful change to what you collect and leave this alone, visitors keep operating under the answer they gave to the old question.
Banner heading and text The wording visitors read. Leave the text empty and it uses the standard wording, which is written to be accurate about what the site does.
Remember their answer for How long before they are asked again. Six months is the default and the usual choice.
Honour the browser's own privacy signal Leave this on. It is a legal requirement in three states and switching it off is a decision to ignore them.
Additional tools to block Only needed if the site starts using a tracking tool the system does not already recognise. It knows about roughly fifty of the common ones, including everything currently on your site. If you ever add something unusual, send it over and it takes a moment to add.

What this does not do

Worth being straight about
  • This is a technical control, not legal advice, and it is not a claim that the site complies with any particular law. It does the things those laws ask a website to do. Whether your business as a whole complies is a question for a lawyer, and depends on things a website cannot see, such as what you do with the enquiries after they arrive.
  • It governs the tools it knows about. That covers the common advertising, analytics, recording and chat tools, and everything currently running on your site, but it is a list rather than magic. A genuinely obscure tool would need adding, which is the setting above.
  • It does not write your privacy policy. The banner links to it, and the policy still has to say what you actually collect and why.

If you want to see it working

Open the site, and when the banner appears choose Reject all. Then open the footer link marked Your Privacy Choices and switch Analytics on. Nothing about the page changes visibly, which is the point: the difference is entirely in what the site is and is not allowed to send elsewhere, and it takes effect immediately rather than on the next page.
